# MY STORIES USER'S GUIDE

This guide helps an author turn a large personal media archive into something family and friends can relive, enjoy, and learn from. It explains a practical method for using the product; it does not replace product truth in `01-Vision-Architecture.md`, `02-Application.md`, or `03-Implementation.md`.

The guide distinguishes three kinds of direction:

- **Rule** — A stable principle that should ordinarily be followed.
- **Default** — A recommended starting choice that works for most archives.
- **Judgment** — A decision that depends on the meaning, sensitivity, or condition of the author's material.

---

## 1. What You Are Building

My Stories is not merely a place to store photographs. It is a way to organize family media, connect it to the stories and context behind it, and deliver those stories privately in a form people can explore and enjoy.

An ordinary photo feed can preserve files and display them by date. My Stories asks the author to do more: identify what the material is about, explain what matters, connect related material, and create useful paths through the archive. That takes judgment. The purpose of the product and this guide is to make that judgment manageable and repeatable rather than leaving every author to invent a method.

### Move through the archive in layers

My Stories is designed to let people navigate, browse, or simply "swim through" their images in whatever ways make sense for their material. The product does not require one complete chronology, one universal hierarchy, or one correct route through a family's history.

The author can add meaning progressively:

1. **Explore Media** — At the simplest level, preserve images and use Who, What, When, and Where Tags to find and consume them more meaningfully than a date-based photo feed ordinarily allows. Captions can add the image-level context that Tags cannot carry.
2. **Create Cards** — Bring one important image or a relevant group of images together when they express a moment, person, event, place, subject, or longer story worth opening.
3. **Build Collections** — Arrange related Cards when intentional selection, context, and order help a reader understand a larger story.

Each level adds an optional layer of authorship. Media remain useful without Cards. Cards remain useful without Collections. A Collection does not replace its Cards, and a Card does not replace its source Media.

**Rule:** Use only as much structure as helps tell the stories the author wants to tell.

### Selective authorship is a feature, not a failure

No level is required to be complete or comprehensive:

- not every Media item must be fully tagged;
- not every Media item needs a caption;
- not every Media item needs to appear in a Card;
- not every Card needs to belong to a Collection; and
- not every family, period, place, or topic needs a finished narrative.

A large archive will ordinarily contain richly documented events, small coherent groups, one-off images, uncertain dates and locations, several family branches, and material whose meaning is not yet understood. The product is intended to accommodate that unevenness rather than pressure the author to disguise it.

The author should concentrate effort where additional context or structure produces additional meaning. A particularly important photograph may justify a detailed caption or its own Card. Twenty nearly self-explanatory photographs may need only broad shared Tags. Unidentified or weakly understood Media may remain in the library until later evidence or interest gives them a useful place.

**Default:** Aim for sufficient understanding, not universal processing. An archive is sufficiently authored when the stories the author cares about can be found, understood, and enjoyed.

### The five parts of an authored archive

- **Media** are the historical evidence: photographs and other source material preserved in one library.
- **Captions** carry concise image-level story: what is shown, why it matters, or what is known about that individual item.
- **Cards** are meaningful units someone may deliberately open. A Card may contain one important image, a sequence of images, or a longer narrative with supporting media.
- **Collections** arrange Cards into authored reading paths without changing or duplicating the Cards themselves.
- **Tags** describe Media and Cards so the same material can be found through Who, What, When, and Where.

**Rule:** Do not ask one part of the system to do another part's job. Tags help people find material; Collections arrange a reading experience; Cards create meaning; captions explain individual Media.

### Tags and Collections group for different reasons

Tags and Collections can both gather related material, but they do not create the same kind of organization:

- A **Tag** records a reusable truth and answers, "What material matches this person, event, life stage, period, or place?"
- A **Collection** records an editorial choice and answers, "What selected Cards should a reader encounter together, and in what context or order?"

For example, `What > Life > Childhood` is useful as a Tag when Childhood is a reusable way to retrieve Media and Cards across several people, families, and periods. A Collection such as `Alan's Childhood`, `Childhood on Cleveland Street`, or `Childhood Across Three Generations` makes a narrower promise: it presents selected Cards as a particular reading experience.

The same concept may therefore appear in both systems when it performs both jobs. The Tag supports broad retrieval; the Collection supplies selection, context, and order. A generic Collection that merely contains everything carrying a Tag usually adds little value.

**Rule:** Use a Tag to say what is true. Build a Collection when intentional selection or sequence helps tell a larger story.

### What the product can and cannot decide

The product can provide import tools, starter vocabulary, filtering, grouping, suggestions, inheritance, and structured authoring surfaces. It can help the author work consistently and can eventually propose more of the organization.

The author remains responsible for deciding which memories matter, what an image or event means, what is uncertain, what is private or sensitive, what deserves its own Card, and what a family member should encounter when reading.

Assistance may suggest, but it must not silently publish, delete, tag, or replace the author's judgment.

### A useful definition of success

A successful archive is not the archive with the most tags, Cards, Collections, or written words. It is one in which the material that matters can be found, selected images have enough context to be understood, Cards feel worth opening, useful reading paths feel coherent, and another person can navigate and enjoy the result without the author standing beside them. Untagged Media, uncarded Media, and uncollected Cards may remain without making the authored parts unsuccessful.

## 2. Start Small

Do not begin by organizing the entire archive. Begin by completing one meaningful part of it.

Large imports create a natural temptation to design the complete taxonomy first or to classify thousands of items before creating any stories. Both approaches delay the point at which the author learns how Media, captions, Cards, Tags, Collections, and Reader presentation affect one another.

### Choose a learning set

**Default:** Begin with approximately 25–100 related images. Choose a group with boundaries the author already understands, such as one trip, one birthday or holiday, one school year or sports season, one person during a bounded period, or one folder or album whose contents are already reasonably coherent.

Avoid choosing the most confusing box of unidentified photographs as the first exercise. It is useful later, but it teaches uncertainty management before the basic authoring cycle is familiar.

### Learn the whole cycle on the bounded set

Take the learning set through the complete authoring path:

1. Import the Media.
2. Review what source evidence is available.
3. Assign useful Who, What, When, and Where Tags.
4. Add captions where individual images need explanation.
5. Decide which Media belong together in Cards.
6. Write only the Card context the material needs.
7. Arrange related Cards in a Collection when a reading path adds value.
8. Review the result in Reader as though encountering it for the first time.

Completing this learning cycle does not mean every item in the bounded set must receive every possible treatment. Some Media may remain lightly tagged, uncaptioned, or outside Cards. The purpose is to experience every authoring level at least once before expanding the taxonomy or accelerating work across a much larger population.

### Work in passes

Trying to perfect every image before moving to the next is slow and inconsistent. A staged approach exposes shared facts and reduces repeated decisions.

**Default:** Work in these passes:

1. **Understand** — Identify the batch, source, approximate period, and obvious shared context.
2. **Classify** — Apply broad shared Tags in bulk.
3. **Refine** — Add exceptions, specific identities, captions, and more detailed Tags only where useful.
4. **Author** — Form Cards and write their necessary context.
5. **Arrange** — Add Cards to Collections where an intentional reading order helps.
6. **Read** — Inspect the result in Reader and correct confusion, fragmentation, or excess.

**Rule:** Import makes Media available; later work adds meaning selectively. Do not confuse availability with authorship, and do not confuse authorship with an obligation to process everything.

### Preserve uncertainty

The author does not need to resolve every fact before making progress. The current product treats `Unknown` and `N/A` as intentional dimensional answers, not as blanks:

- **Unknown** means that the dimension applies but the answer is not currently known.
- **N/A** means that the dimension is not meaningfully applicable to the item.
- **Blank** means that the dimension has not yet been reviewed.

**Rule:** Do not convert a guess into confirmed archive truth merely to make an item look complete.

## 3. Design Your Tags

Tags create the common descriptive language used by both Media and Cards. A useful taxonomy contains enough detail to retrieve meaningful material without attempting to encode every fact or relationship.

The four dimensions are:

- **Who** — Stable people and named animals shown in or central to the material.
- **What** — Events, activities, topics, objects, and reflections.
- **When** — Sortable chronology at the level actually known or useful.
- **Where** — Geographic places and meaningful venues.

### Begin with retrieval, not description

Before creating a Tag, ask: **Will I or a reader use this distinction to find, filter, group, understand, or author material differently?**

If the answer is no, the fact may belong in a caption or Card narrative rather than in the taxonomy.

**Rule:** Create a Tag because it improves repeated use of the archive, not merely because a detail can be named.

### Start broad and earn detail

**Default:** Begin with stable parent concepts and add children only when the archive contains enough material to make the distinction useful.

For example, `Baseball` may initially be sufficient. Add a team, season, league, field, or tournament only when that distinction helps retrieve or present a meaningful population. A taxonomy designed for hypothetical future material becomes harder to understand and apply consistently.

Useful child Tags should represent distinctions the author expects to assign directly or use as filters. Purely navigational parents may organize the hierarchy, but unnecessary layers add authoring burden without improving the Reader experience.

### Use one stable identity

Who represents stable people and named animals used as subjects. A person should not become several unrelated identities because photographs use married names, maiden names, nicknames, initials, or imported variants. Names and variants describe one identity; they should not silently fragment that person's archive.

The Who hierarchy may use meaningful branches such as Family or Friends and may preserve useful nested name variants. Parent, spouse, cousin, grandmother, and similar perspective-relative relationships are not assignable identity Tags. They depend on the viewer's perspective and belong in optional relationship facts rather than in the descriptive taxonomy.

**Rule:** One real subject should have one stable identity even when that subject has multiple names.

Employers, schools, teams, clubs, and other named organizations do not become Who Tags merely because they matter to a story. Use the person's Who Tag, add a reusable broad What concept when it improves retrieval, and preserve the organization's specific name in the Card title, caption, or narrative.

For example, a Card about Alan's time at McKesson can use `Who > Alan` and `What > Topics > Business`. If the story is specifically about Alan's working life, `What > Life > Career` may also be useful. `McKesson` belongs in the title or narrative unless repeated archive use later proves that a dedicated concept materially improves retrieval.

### Use a compact What vocabulary

Start with six broad What branches:

- **Life** — Childhood, Parenthood, Education, Career, and Marriage. Education may use Preschool, Elementary, Middle School, High School, College, and Graduate children when useful.
- **Events** — Birth, Birthday, Engagement, Wedding, Anniversary, Graduation, Funeral, Reunion, Party, and Holidays. Holidays is assignable and may contain useful named holidays.
- **Activities** — Sports, Hobbies, Clubs, Travel, Recreation, Performing, and Building. Sports is assignable and may contain useful individual sports.
- **Topics** — Business, Culture, Technology, Politics, Music, Books, Movies, Television, Food, and Ancestry.
- **Objects** — Homes, Vehicles, Clothing, and Machinery.
- **Reflections** — Outcomes, Lessons, Superlatives, and Future.

These are useful starting points, not boxes that must all be filled. Meaningful parents such as Education, Holidays, Sports, and Reflections are assignable. Add a child only when the narrower population will be repeatedly useful.

### Allow controlled multiple What Tags

Use one primary What subject. Most items should need one to three What Tags, normally no more than one from the same branch. A child already contributes to its parent when descendant filtering is used, so do not assign both simply to reproduce the hierarchy.

Multiple What Tags are appropriate when they describe independent, reusable facets. A college-football engagement might use `Life > Education > College`, `Activities > Sports > Football`, and `Events > Engagement`. The university, team, date, and other occurrence-specific detail can remain in the title, caption, When, or Where.

**Rule:** Tag for repeated retrieval; write the particulars into the story.

### Distinguish assignment from subject

A Card or Media item can have several Tags in a dimension. A **subject** marks which assigned Tag or Tags deserve emphasis; it is not a fifth dimension and it does not replace ordinary assignments.

For example, a photograph may include several people while principally being about one person. Assign the people who are meaningfully present, then mark the principal person as the Who subject when that distinction improves presentation or filtering.

**Default:** Use subjects deliberately. Do not mark every assigned Tag as a subject merely because the control is available.

### Media truth and Card truth are different

Media Tags describe what is true of an individual image. Card Tags describe what is true of the authored story or Gallery as a whole.

A photograph of Alan at a baseball game may accurately carry Alan, Baseball, a date, and a ballpark. A Card using that photograph to tell a broader story about fatherhood may need additional or different What Tags because the story's meaning is not fully visible in the image.

Gallery inheritance can roll confirmed Media Tags into selected Card dimensions, but each dimension requires an explicit author choice and existing Card assignments remain protected. Inheritance accelerates repeated truth; it does not eliminate Card-level judgment.

**Rule:** Do not change source-Media truth merely to make a Card easier to classify.

### Use Unknown, N/A, and blank consistently

Each dimension needs three distinct states:

- use a known Tag when the answer is known;
- use the dimension's `Unknown` Tag when an answer exists but is not known;
- use the dimension's `N/A` Tag when the dimension does not meaningfully apply;
- leave the dimension blank only while it remains unreviewed.

This distinction allows Complete and Incomplete workflows to reflect real authoring progress without disguising uncertainty.

### What not to encode as Tags

Avoid creating Tags for temporary workflow states such as `Needs review` when the product already tracks completeness; cover, body, or Gallery placement, which are Media relationships; one-time observations better expressed in a caption; arbitrary combinations of people, which filters or Collections can assemble; perspective-relative family roles used as identities; or distinctions so fine that the author cannot apply them consistently.

### Use Tag Set 0 as a starting point

Tag Set 0 is an optional, additive starter vocabulary. It provides common family roles, topics, dates, and United States locations without assigning anything automatically. It is conflict-safe and removable by its install identity.

**Default:** A new author should inspect Tag Set 0 before designing a taxonomy from scratch, retain the concepts that fit the archive, and extend it only in response to real material.

The starter set is not a finished taxonomy for every family. Its purpose is to reduce blank-page design work while preserving author control.

### Review the taxonomy before scaling

After completing the first learning set, review Tags that were difficult to choose between, Tags never assigned, branches with only one useful child, identities that may be duplicated under different names, details repeatedly placed in captions instead of Tags, and searches or groupings the author expected but could not perform.

Only then expand the taxonomy for the next body of material.

## 4. Import and Prepare Media

Media enters a private author library. Importing an image does not publish it, place it in a Story, or expose the rest of the library to readers.

On a computer, use the Studio Media Library to bring in selected files or a bounded folder. On a phone, Start a Story can select device photos for that Story. The product must retain its own usable copy of Media that becomes part of the archive; Apple Photos or Google Photos may remain the place where an author initially finds it.

Before a large import, preserve the best source file available and correct trustworthy embedded facts when practical. Scanned images often need an author-supplied date, location, caption, or identity because the scan date is not the historical date. Phone images ordinarily provide stronger date and location evidence, but machine metadata is evidence rather than unquestionable truth.

After import, confirm that the files appear, inspect processing failures, and use Batch, Folder, Metadata, or Day grouping when it helps review the new population. A pending or failed rendition is a processing condition, not permission to delete the source.

If a scan or photo is corrected in another editor, open that existing Media item and choose **Replace image** instead of importing the edited file as a new item. Compare the current and proposed images, check their filenames, dimensions, and sizes, and confirm replacement only when they are the same underlying photo or scan. Replacement keeps the Media caption, tags, review decisions, and Story relationships. A different photo belongs as a new Media item; an exact file already owned by another item belongs in duplicate review. If the source is accepted but a rendition or search stage fails, the replacement remains visible as processing work that can be retried.

**Default:** Import one understandable population, confirm that it is usable, then classify or author from it before adding another large population.

## 5. Tag Effectively

Settings offers four archive standards: require neither Media nor Cards to have complete Who, What, When, and Where review; require Media only; require Cards only; or require both. These are author-selected standards, not different products.

Draft work remains open under every standard. The selected requirements are enforced when publishing a Card. If Media review is required, the Media referenced by that Card must be reviewed; unrelated library Media does not block the Story. If Card review is required, the Card must have all four dimensions reviewed and a meaningful What. Blank remains unreviewed; Unknown and N/A are explicit searchable decisions.

Work from broad shared truth toward exceptions:

1. Select a bounded population using a batch, folder, date, or existing Tag.
2. Apply shared Tags in bulk.
3. Remove or refine Tags for exceptions.
4. Add subjects only where emphasis helps.
5. Use Incomplete and dimensional review filters to find the remaining decisions.

**Rule:** Never use Unknown or N/A merely to clear a queue. Use them only when they truthfully describe what the author knows.

## 6. Turn Media Into Stories

Start with the reason another person should open the Story. Select the images, then supply only the words needed to make their meaning understandable.

The phone Story flow separates the work into five manageable steps:

1. **Words** — Give the Story a useful title and write the narrative it needs.
2. **Media** — Choose the cover and supporting images; add or improve image captions where they carry important image-level context.
3. **Details** — Review the Story's Who, What, When, and Where meaning and choose its audience.
4. **Placement** — Decide whether the Story belongs in Feed, one or more Collection locations, or both.
5. **Preview** — Read the Story as a reader will encounter it before publishing.

A Story may be published in Feed without belonging to a Collection. It may instead appear through a Collection, or use both routes. Placement does not duplicate the Story; each placement points to the same canonical Card.

Draft saving is permissive. Publishing requires the selected classification standard, valid referenced Media, ready renditions, an explicit nonempty audience, and at least one Reader route. Audience can use all enabled readers, reusable groups, or selected individuals.

At the current stage, a published Card is one live object. Saved edits may become visible to readers immediately. Return it to Draft before making changes that must remain private until republished.

**Default:** Complete and share one modest Story before building a comprehensive Collection.

## 7. Build Collections

A Collection is an authored reading path, not a second copy of the archive. Begin it as a private draft and add only the structure that improves understanding.

Use Parts for the broadest divisions, Chapters for substantial movements within the work, and Sections only where a Chapter benefits from another meaningful layer. An ordinary Story may also sit directly at Collection level. The same Story may appear in more than one location when each placement serves a real reading purpose.

Structure can be created before every Story is written. Add Part, Chapter, or Section creates a title-only draft Story at the chosen structural location; open that same Card later to add introductory content. Removing a placement does not delete the Card.

Collection publication is separate from Card publication. A Collection must have an explicit audience, valid structure, and no missing or draft required Cards. Published structure is locked until the Collection deliberately returns to Draft.

**Rule:** Do not publish `My Life` or another large Collection merely to see what it looks like. Use Preview while readiness work remains unfinished.

## 8. Review the Reader Experience

Reader Home offers Collections and Feed. Collections provide authored sequence and context; Feed supports direct and freeform exploration. A direct authorized Story link may bypass both entrances.

Review with the intended reader in mind:

- Can the reader tell where to begin?
- Does the title make a clear promise?
- Do captions explain images that would otherwise be ambiguous?
- Does the Story contain enough context without repeating what the images already show?
- Does Collection placement improve understanding?
- Can the reader return, continue, and recognize where they are?
- Does the selected audience match the author's intent?

Test representative phone and desktop reading. Reader access includes only authorized published Cards and Collections plus the Media those surfaces reference. It does not expose the author's private Media bank.

Publishing and delivery are not the same action. The author can authorize password accounts, guest recipients, groups, and individuals. In User Management, review the access a new guest will inherit, create the guest, and manually send the private invitation link. The link works once and expires after seven days; use **New invitation link** if it must be replaced. Automated email distribution, password recovery, and conversion from guest access to a password account remain future capabilities.

**Default:** Preview first, publish to the smallest truthful audience, and then read the result from that audience's route.

## 9. Maintain the Archive

An archive remains useful when its source files, authored meaning, access rules, and recovery path stay trustworthy.

Review these conditions periodically:

- failed or pending Media processing;
- unresolved dimensional review that matters to the selected classification standard;
- missing Media references;
- failed durable operations;
- audience groups whose membership has changed;
- published Cards whose current audience or placement no longer matches the author's intent; and
- Collections whose structure or required Cards are unfinished.

Use the owning surface for each correction. Media truth belongs in the Media Library, Card meaning belongs in Compose, Collection order belongs in Collections, and reader membership belongs in User Management. Dashboard and Needs Attention counts are evidence and navigation aids; they do not authorize automatic repair.

Before a broad migration or destructive cleanup, create and verify a recoverable backup, inspect an exact impact preview, make one bounded change through the authoritative operation, and read the result back independently. Tag removal or merge, audience-group membership changes, publication transitions, and Media deletion all deserve consequence review.

Published Cards currently remain live objects. If a revision must remain private, return the Card to Draft before editing. Do not assume that an unfinished private working-revision system protects published content.

**Rule:** Prefer a small, verified correction over a bulk action whose exceptions cannot be reviewed.

## 10. Worked Examples

### A phone-first birthday Story

Elizabeth wants to share Charlie's recent birthday. Her phone already makes the photographs easy to find by date and face, so she does not need to import or classify her whole camera roll.

1. From Author Home, choose Start a Story.
2. In Media, select only the birthday photographs that belong in the Story.
3. In Words, title the Story and add the context the photographs do not convey.
4. Caption only the images whose meaning or participants would otherwise be unclear.
5. In Details, follow the archive's selected classification standard and choose the intended family audience.
6. In Placement, include the Story in Feed, a family Collection, or both.
7. Preview on a phone, then publish when the audience and route are correct.

This workflow produces one durable Story and retains the Media it uses. It does not require importing, tagging, or managing every photograph already on the phone. If classification is optional, search and discovery will rely more heavily on the Story's words, captions, and whatever confirmed Tags the author elects to add.

### A scanned family-history batch

Alan has a folder of scanned photographs inherited across several generations. The file dates mostly describe scanning rather than the historical events, so the source needs more deliberate preparation.

1. Choose one understandable folder of approximately 25–100 images.
2. Preserve the best scans and import that bounded folder into the Media Library.
3. Group by Folder or Batch and identify shared people, period, place, and event context.
4. Apply broad shared Who, What, When, and Where truth in bulk, then correct exceptions.
5. Use structured dates at the precision actually known; use Unknown only where the historical answer cannot presently be established.
6. Add captions to important individual photographs, especially where identity, provenance, or significance is not visually evident.
7. Create several focused Stories rather than one enormous record.
8. Arrange those Stories in a draft family-history Collection when sequence and introduction improve understanding.
9. Review the Collection in Preview while missing references, unfinished Cards, or audience decisions remain.
10. Publish Stories and eventually the Collection only after their selected classification, Media readiness, audience, and destination requirements are satisfied.

This workflow uses the full archive benefit: author-confirmed hierarchy and dimensions support retrieval across people, events, time, and place. The additional precision is valuable because the scanned source lacks much of the searchable context a modern phone library ordinarily provides.

### What the examples have in common

Both authors preserve only the Media they deliberately bring into My Stories, create Cards as the durable units of meaning, and control who may read published work. The difference is the amount of archive organization they need. Phone-first authors can begin with one Story and light classification. Archival authors can invest in complete Media and Card review where that precision improves freeform exploration and long-term understanding.
